# Deal-structure selection rules

Practitioner + transaction-cost-economics rules for choosing which
partnership vehicle (revenue share, licensing, reseller, joint venture,
co-marketing) fits a given deal, and when to default to the simplest
vehicle instead of reaching for a heavier one.

## Decision rules

### 1. Pick governance structure by asset specificity and transaction frequency, not by deal size
- **Condition**: when structuring a proposed deal and the partner relationship requires either party to make investments specialized to this specific counterpart (co-built integration, dedicated support team, jointly-developed IP) or the transaction will recur at high frequency **Choice**: as asset specificity and recurrence rise, move governance along the spectrum market (simple purchase/reseller agreement) -> hybrid (revenue share, licensing with ongoing royalty) -> hierarchy (joint venture, equity stake); do not default to a JV or equity structure for a low-specificity, low-frequency, one-off transaction just because the deal is large in dollar terms **Why**: Williamson's transaction cost economics shows asset specificity creates bilateral dependency and hold-up risk that only tighter (more hierarchical) governance can safeguard against, while standardized, infrequent transactions are efficiently governed through markets with no added structure — meta-analytic review found strong empirical support for this alignment on both make-vs-buy and ally-vs-buy decisions **Source**: Tadelis, S. & Williamson, O., "Transaction Cost Economics", https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/stadelis/tce_org_handbook_111410.pdf ; academic meta-analysis, "Make, Buy, or Ally: A Transaction Cost Theory Meta-Analysis", Academy of Management Journal, https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amj.2006.21794670 **Counter-example test**: a one-time, standardized-product reseller deal escalated straight to a 50/50 joint-venture entity (new legal entity, shared board seats) because the first-year revenue projection is large fails this rule — deal size alone never overrides low asset-specificity/low-frequency signals.

### 2. Default to the lightest vehicle that accomplishes the stated objective; escalate only when a lighter option provably cannot
- **Condition**: when more than one deal vehicle (supply agreement, distribution agreement, licensing, revenue share, joint venture) could plausibly achieve the partnership's stated business objective **Choice**: rank candidate vehicles from lightest (simple commercial agreement, least shared control/complexity) to heaviest (joint venture, new legal entity), and select the lightest vehicle that the objective's own requirements do not rule out; require an explicit, written reason before selecting anything heavier than the lightest viable option **Why**: a joint venture or other heavy structure should only be pursued if it is a better vehicle than the available lighter alternatives — if any lighter option accomplishes the objective with similar results, practitioner guidance is to prefer it, since shared control and joint-entity overhead are themselves ongoing transaction costs **Source**: Ankura Joint Ventures and Partnerships, "How to Structure a Joint Venture: The Five Essential Elements of JV Dealmaking", https://jvalchemist.ankura.com/transactions/how-to-structure-a-joint-venture-the-five-essential-elements-of-jv-dealmaking/ **Counter-example test**: choosing a joint venture for a partnership whose only stated objective is "co-sell into each other's customer base" — an objective a revenue-share or reseller agreement already accomplishes — fails this rule without a documented reason the lighter option can't meet the objective.

### 3. Remove a revenue-share structure once its "revenue" definition disputes recur, rather than layering clarifying amendments onto it
- **Condition**: when a live revenue-share agreement has generated more than one dispute or clarification request over what counts as shared "revenue" (services fees, upsells, renewals, discounts) **Choice**: do not respond to a second ambiguity dispute by adding another clarifying amendment to the existing revenue-share clause; instead treat recurring ambiguity as a structural signal and drop the revenue-share mechanism for a fixed-fee, flat-license, or per-unit-commission structure that removes the ambiguous "what counts as revenue" question entirely **Why**: the most common source of disputes in revenue-share arrangements is ambiguity about what counts as revenue; each added clarifying amendment is an additive fix to a structural problem, and teams systematically reach for addition (one more amendment) over the subtractive option (drop the ambiguous mechanism) even when removal is the more effective fix **Source**: practitioner synthesis on revenue-share disputes, UpCounsel, "Revenue Sharing Agreement Basics and Legal Considerations", https://www.upcounsel.com/revenue-share-partnership-agreement ; subtraction-neglect mechanism: Adams, G.S., Converse, B.A., Hales, A.H., Klotz, L.E., "People systematically overlook subtractive changes", Nature 592, 258-261 (2021), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03380-y **Counter-example test**: a revenue-share deal now on its third "revenue definition" amendment rider, with the underlying % split clause untouched, fails this rule — the pattern itself (recurring definitional disputes answered only by addition) is the violation, independent of whether any single amendment is well-drafted.
