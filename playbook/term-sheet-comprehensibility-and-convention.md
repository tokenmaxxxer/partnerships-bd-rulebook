# Term-sheet comprehensibility and convention rules

Practitioner + cognitive-psychology-of-reading rules for drafting a
term sheet that both sides' non-lawyer stakeholders can actually parse,
and for using market-standard structure over bespoke structure by
default.

## Decision rules

### 1. Use the market-standard section order and naming (purpose, roles, terms, governance, KPIs, dispute resolution, exit) instead of a bespoke structure
- **Condition**: when laying out a new term sheet or deal-summary document for a partnership **Choice**: follow the section order and naming convention that market-standard model documents (NVCA-family term sheets and comparable model docs) already establish for the corresponding deal type, rather than inventing a new section order or merging distinct concerns (e.g., governance and KPIs) into one section for this deal alone **Why**: standardized documents like the NVCA forms exist precisely so that a counterpart's counsel and business team can navigate the document by prior familiarity with the convention; a bespoke structure forces every reader to re-derive where each concern lives, adding avoidable comprehension cost with no offsetting benefit for a deal that isn't unusual enough to require it **Source**: VC Beast, "NVCA Forms: 6 Free Model Documents Explained", https://vcbeast.com/nvca-forms-model-documents-guide **Counter-example test**: a term sheet that buries exit/termination terms as a sub-bullet inside the "Terms" section instead of giving it its own named section, deviating from convention with no stated reason, fails this rule.

### 2. Replace low-frequency, center-embedded legal phrasing with plain, front-loaded sentence structure in any clause a non-lawyer stakeholder must approve
- **Condition**: when drafting or reviewing a term-sheet clause that a business-side stakeholder (not counsel) is expected to read and approve on their own understanding **Choice**: rewrite center-embedded constructions ("the Company shall, except as otherwise provided in Section 4.2 hereof, be obligated to...") into front-loaded, single-clause sentences with the actor and obligation stated first, and replace low-frequency legal vocabulary with its plain-language equivalent wherever a plain equivalent exists without losing legal precision **Why**: readability research on contracts found that words difficult to anticipate from context and center-embedded sentence structure measurably increase cognitive load and reduce comprehension and recall, and that removing archaic/redundant terms and simplifying sentence structure improves non-expert comprehension even though absolute comprehension levels remain a persistent challenge **Source**: ScienceDirect, "Poor writing, not specialized concepts, drives processing difficulty in legal language", https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027722000580 ; Masson & Waldron, "Comprehension of legal contracts by non-experts: Effectiveness of plain language redrafting", Applied Cognitive Psychology, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.2350080107 **Counter-example test**: a KPI clause a business sponsor is asked to sign off on, written as one 90-word sentence with two nested exception clauses before the main obligation appears, fails this rule even if every word in it is individually plain.

### 3. Cut a term-sheet clause that restates or duplicates another section rather than leaving both in "for completeness"
- **Condition**: when a term-sheet draft review finds that a clause restates a commitment, definition, or condition already stated in another section (e.g., a KPI threshold repeated inside both the "Terms" and "Governance" sections with slightly different wording) **Choice**: delete the duplicate and consolidate the commitment into the single section that owns that concern, rather than keeping both "for completeness" or "in case someone only reads one section"; if cross-reference is genuinely needed, use an explicit pointer to the owning section instead of a restated duplicate **Why**: comprehensibility research on legal drafting attributes processing difficulty to document-level features, not just word choice, and duplicated or redundant terms are a named target of plain-language redrafting for exactly this reason — every duplicate is also a divergence risk (the two copies drift out of sync over amendments) that a single owning section avoids; drafters default to leaving the duplicate in because deleting an already-written clause is the subtractive option, systematically the one people are least likely to choose **Source**: Statute Law Review, "'It's Complicated': the development of plain language legislative drafting in Australia, Canada, and New Zealand", https://academic.oup.com/slr/article/47/1/hmag004/8539984 ; subtraction-neglect mechanism: Adams, G.S., Converse, B.A., Hales, A.H., Klotz, L.E., "People systematically overlook subtractive changes", Nature 592, 258-261 (2021), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03380-y **Counter-example test**: a term sheet where the KPI threshold appears as "95% uptime" in the Terms section and "uptime SLA of 95%, measured monthly" in the Governance section, with no cross-reference and no single owning section, fails this rule — the two copies are a latent drift risk independent of whether they currently agree.
